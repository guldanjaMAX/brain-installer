# What your brain can answer, and what it cannot

One page. Keep it near you for the first month.

The rule underneath all of it: **your brain reads what your business already wrote down.** It is very good at finding something that exists and telling you where it came from. It is not a calculator, it is not your accountant, and it is not a decision maker.

---

## Five questions it answers well

Swap in your own names.

**1. "What did we agree to deliver for [client], and by when?"**
The commitment is sitting in a proposal, an email, or a call transcript. You know it exists. You just do not know which of four hundred documents it is in. That is exactly the job.

**2. "Why did we stop working with [vendor]?"**
Nobody ever wrote a summary of this. But the reasoning is scattered across a thread, a meeting, and a note to yourself, and it can pull the pieces together and show you each one.

**3. "What has [person] asked for more than once?"**
A pattern across many documents. A human cannot see this by scrolling, because the three requests are eight months apart in three different places. This is the shape of question that is worth the most and gets asked the least.

**4. "What did we quote for the last three jobs like this one?"**
It finds the comparable jobs and cites each quote separately, so you can see the actual documents rather than trusting a number.

**5. "What is still open from the [month] meeting with [person]?"**
If the transcript exists, it will pull the open items. If the transcript does not exist, it tells you that instead of guessing, which is the second most useful answer available.

**What those five have in common:** somebody, at some point, wrote the answer down in words. Finding it and citing it is the whole job.

---

## Three questions it answers badly

Not because it fails. Because it will produce something that looks like an answer.

### 1. "How much did we make last month?"

**Why this goes wrong:** that is arithmetic across a system of record. Your brain reads documents; it does not add up your books. Ask it anyway and it will find a document containing a revenue number and quote it to you with a citation attached, which looks authoritative. That number could be a forecast, a quote you never won, or a draft from March.

**A cited wrong number is worse than no answer**, because you have no reason to doubt it.

**Ask instead:** "what did we invoice [client] for in [month]", then check it against the system that actually owns the money.

### 2. "Should I raise my prices?"

**Why this goes wrong:** that is a judgment, and judgment is not retrieval. It can tell you what you have charged, what clients said about price, and when you last changed it. It cannot decide, and it has no stake in the outcome.

If the answer comes back sounding decisive, that confidence is the writing engine, not your files.

**Ask instead:** "what have clients said about my pricing" and "when did we last change our rates and why". Then make the call yourself, better informed.

### 3. "What did [person] say on the call yesterday?"

**Why this goes wrong:** two problems at once.

First, it only knows what was written down. If that call was not recorded or transcribed, there is nothing to find, and no amount of rephrasing will produce it.

Second, yesterday may not be loaded yet. Your material syncs on a schedule, not instantly, and something from yesterday afternoon may not be in there this morning.

**Ask instead:** first "do we have a transcript from [date] with [person]", then ask about its contents. That way an empty answer means "it never existed" rather than leaving you guessing which of the two problems you hit.

## How to tell a real answer from a confident one

**Look for the brackets.** Every factual claim carries a number like `[3]`, and the numbers map to the sources listed below the answer. Text with no brackets is not coming from your files.

**Read the gaps.** The list underneath the answer is not decoration. It is the system telling you what it could not see. "Only 2 sources matched, treat this as a weak signal" changes how much weight the answer deserves.

**A line starting "Heads up:"** means something about the evidence should change your confidence. It appears only when it matters, so when it appears, it matters.

**"The documents do not answer this" is a real answer.** It is the one thing you cannot get from anything else in this category, and it is the reason the rest can be trusted. A tool that always produces something has taught you nothing about when to believe it.

---

## Three rules of thumb

1. **If someone had to write it down for you to know it, your brain can find it. If not, it cannot.** Decisions made in a hallway are gone.

2. **If the answer needs adding, averaging, or counting across records, ask the system that owns those records.** Accounting questions go to accounting.

3. **If the answer is a decision, it is yours.** The brain's job is to make sure you are making it with everything you already knew, instead of only what you remembered.
